🛠️ 1. Work

Work is said to be done by a force when the body is displaced actually through some distance in the direction of the applied force.

Formula: W = F • d = F d cosθ

  • Positive Work: If 0° ≤ θ < 90°. Example: Work done by gravity on a freely falling body.
  • Negative Work: If 90° < θ ≤ 180°. Example: Work done by friction.
  • Zero Work: If θ = 90° or d = 0. Example: Work done by centripetal force is zero because force is perpendicular to velocity.
💡 Work done by Variable Force

Work done by a variable force is given by the integral of force over the displacement: W = ∫ F(x) dx. Geometrically, it is the area under the Force-Displacement graph.

⚡ 2. Energy & Work-Energy Theorem

Energy is the capacity to do work. It is a scalar quantity and its SI unit is Joule (J).

Kinetic Energy (K)

Energy possessed by a body by virtue of its motion. K = ½mv2.

Relation between Kinetic Energy and Momentum (p): K = p2 / 2m.

Work-Energy Theorem

The net work done by all the forces (conservative and non-conservative) on a body is equal to the change in its kinetic energy.

Wnet = Kfinal - Kinitial = ΔK

🚨 Exam Facts: Conservative Forces

Work done by a conservative force (like gravity, spring force, electrostatic force) depends only on initial and final positions and is independent of the path taken. Work done by a conservative force in a closed loop is zero.

🔋 3. Potential Energy

Energy possessed by a body by virtue of its position or configuration. It is defined only for conservative forces. Change in potential energy ΔU = - Wconservative.

  • Gravitational Potential Energy: U = mgh (near Earth's surface).
  • Elastic Potential Energy of a Spring: U = ½kx2 (where k is spring constant and x is extension or compression).

Law of Conservation of Mechanical Energy

If only conservative forces are doing work on a system, the total mechanical energy (K + U) remains constant. Einitial = Efinal.

💥 4. Collisions

A strong interaction between bodies that occurs for a short time interval. Total momentum is conserved in all types of collisions.

Elastic Collision

Both momentum and kinetic energy are conserved. The forces involved are conservative.

In a 1-D elastic collision, velocity of separation equals velocity of approach: v2 - v1 = u1 - u2.

💡 Velocities after 1D Elastic Collision

v1 = [(m1 - m2)/(m1 + m2)]u1 + [2m2/(m1 + m2)]u2
If masses are equal (m1 = m2), the bodies simply exchange their velocities.

Inelastic Collision

Momentum is conserved but kinetic energy is NOT conserved (some is lost as heat/sound). Perfectly inelastic collision: the bodies stick together after impact and move with a common velocity.

Coefficient of Restitution (e)

e = (Relative velocity of separation) / (Relative velocity of approach).
For perfect elastic: e = 1.
For perfect inelastic: e = 0.
For inelastic: 0 < e < 1.

⏱️ 5. Power

Power is defined as the time rate at which work is done or energy is transferred.

  • Average Power: Pavg = W / t.
  • Instantaneous Power: P = dW/dt. Since dW = F • dr, we get P = F • v (dot product of force and velocity).

Unit of power is Watt (W). 1 HP (Horse Power) = 746 W.

💡 Short Trick: Relation K and p

If kinetic energy increases by x%, use the exact formula for momentum change. But if the change is very small (< 5%), use the error approximation: % change in p = ½ × (% change in K).
