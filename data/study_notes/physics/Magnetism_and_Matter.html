🧲 1. The Bar Magnet

A bar magnet has two poles (North and South) of equal pole strength (m). Magnetic field lines emerge from the North pole and enter the South pole. Inside the magnet, they go from South to North, forming continuous closed loops.

Magnetic Dipole Moment (M)

M = m × 2l

Direction: From South pole to North pole.

💡 Torque & Potential Energy

When placed in a uniform magnetic field B:

  • Torque: τ = M × B = MB sinθ
  • Potential Energy: U = -M • B = -MB cosθ

🌍 2. Earth's Magnetism

The earth behaves as a huge magnet. The magnetic field of the earth is specified by three elements at any place:

  1. Magnetic Declination (θ): The angle between true geographic north and the magnetic north (direction of compass needle).
  2. Angle of Dip or Inclination (δ): The angle made by the earth's total magnetic field B with the horizontal direction in the magnetic meridian. At poles, δ = 90°. At equator, δ = 0°.
  3. Horizontal Component of Earth's Magnetic Field (BH): BH = B cosδ. Vertical component BV = B sinδ. So, tanδ = BV / BH.

🧲 3. Magnetic Properties of Materials

Materials are classified based on their response to an external magnetic field into three categories:

1. Diamagnetic Materials

Develop feeble magnetization in the opposite direction to the applied field. They are feebly repelled by magnets. Examples: Copper, Bismuth, Water, Gold.

Susceptibility (χ): Small and negative. Independent of temperature.

2. Paramagnetic Materials

Develop feeble magnetization in the direction of the applied field. They are feebly attracted by magnets. Examples: Aluminum, Sodium, Oxygen.

Susceptibility (χ): Small and positive. Inversely proportional to absolute temperature (Curie's Law: χ ∝ 1/T).

3. Ferromagnetic Materials

Develop strong magnetization in the direction of the applied field. They are strongly attracted by magnets. Examples: Iron, Cobalt, Nickel, Gadolinium.

Susceptibility (χ): Very large and positive. Form magnetic domains. Above Curie temperature (TC), they become paramagnetic.

📈 4. Hysteresis

The lagging of magnetic induction (B) behind the magnetizing field (H) when a ferromagnetic material is taken through a cycle of magnetization.

  • Retentivity: The value of magnetic induction B left in the specimen when the magnetizing field H is reduced to zero.
  • Coercivity: The reverse magnetizing field H required to completely destroy the residual magnetism.
🚨 Exam Facts: Choice of Material
  • Permanent Magnets: Require High Retentivity and High Coercivity. (e.g., Steel, Alnico).
  • Electromagnets & Transformer Cores: Require High Permeability, Low Retentivity, and Low Coercivity to minimize hysteresis loss (area of loop). (e.g., Soft Iron).

🔄 5. Gauss's Law for Magnetism

The net magnetic flux through any closed surface is always zero.

∮ B • dA = 0

📘 Physical Significance

This law establishes that isolated magnetic poles (monopoles) do not exist. Magnetic poles always exist in pairs (N and S).
