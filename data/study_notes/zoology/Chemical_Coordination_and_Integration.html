🧪 1. Endocrine Glands and Hormones

Endocrine glands lack ducts and are hence, called ductless glands. Their secretions are called hormones.

📘 Definition: Hormones

Hormones are non-nutrient chemicals which act as intercellular messengers and are produced in trace amounts.

Hypothalamus

Located at the base of the diencephalon (forebrain). It contains neurosecretory cells called nuclei which produce hormones. These hormones regulate the synthesis and secretion of pituitary hormones. Types: releasing hormones (e.g., GnRH) and inhibiting hormones (e.g., Somatostatin).

👑 2. Pituitary and Pineal Glands

Pituitary Gland (The Master Gland)

Located in a bony cavity called sella tursica. Divided anatomically into an adenohypophysis and a neurohypophysis.

RegionHormones SecretedMajor Functions
Pars Distalis (Anterior Pituitary)GH, PRL, TSH, ACTH, LH, FSHGrowth, Milk production, Thyroid stimulation, Adrenal cortex stimulation, Gonad stimulation.
Pars IntermediaMSH (Melanocyte Stimulating Hormone)Acts on melanocytes, regulates pigmentation of the skin.
Pars Nervosa (Posterior Pituitary)Oxytocin, Vasopressin (ADH)Synthesized by hypothalamus and transported here. Oxytocin helps in child birth (uterine contraction) and milk ejection. ADH reabsorbs water in kidneys.

Pineal Gland

Located on the dorsal side of forebrain. Secretes a hormone called melatonin. Melatonin plays a very important role in the regulation of a 24-hour (diurnal) rhythm of our body (sleep-wake cycle, body temperature).

🚨 Exam Facts: Pituitary Disorders

Gigantism: Over-secretion of GH during childhood.
Acromegaly: Over-secretion of GH in adults (especially middle age) leading to severe disfigurement (especially face).
Dwarfism: Low secretion of GH.
Diabetes Insipidus: Impairment affecting synthesis/release of ADH, resulting in water loss and dehydration.

🦋 3. Thyroid and Parathyroid Glands

Thyroid Gland

Located on either side of the trachea. Composed of follicles and stromal tissues. Synthesizes two main hormones: T4 (thyroxine) and T3. Iodine is essential for their normal rate of synthesis. Functions include regulation of BMR, RBC formation, control of metabolism of carbohydrates, proteins and fats. It also secretes TCT (Thyrocalcitonin) which regulates blood calcium levels.

  • Hypothyroidism: Enlargement of thyroid gland (Goitre). During pregnancy, it causes defective development of growing baby (Cretinism: stunted growth, mental retardation). In adult women, may cause irregular menstrual cycle.
  • Hyperthyroidism: Exophthalmic goitre (Grave's disease) characterised by enlargement of thyroid gland, protrusion of eyeballs, increased BMR, and weight loss.

Parathyroid Gland

Four parathyroid glands are present on the back side of the thyroid gland. They secrete Parathyroid hormone (PTH).

Role of PTH: It is a hypercalcemic hormone, meaning it increases blood Ca²⁺ levels. It acts on bones (stimulates bone resorption/demineralisation), stimulates reabsorption of Ca²⁺ by renal tubules and increases Ca²⁺ absorption from the digested food.

🤯 Amazing Info: Antagonistic Action

PTH and TCT (Thyrocalcitonin) have exactly opposite actions! While PTH increases blood calcium by pulling it from bones, TCT decreases blood calcium by putting it back into the bones.

⚔️ 4. Adrenal and Pancreas

Adrenal Gland

Located at the anterior part of each kidney. Composed of two types of tissues: centrally located adrenal medulla and outside adrenal cortex.

  • Adrenal Medulla: Secretes Adrenaline (epinephrine) and Noradrenaline (norepinephrine). These are called Emergency hormones or hormones of Fight or Flight. They increase alertness, pupillary dilation, piloerection (raising of hairs), sweating, heart beat, and breakdown of glycogen (increasing blood glucose).
  • Adrenal Cortex: Secretes corticoids.
    • Glucocorticoids (e.g., Cortisol): Involved in carbohydrate metabolism. Stimulate gluconeogenesis, lipolysis and proteolysis. Cortisol is also anti-inflammatory and suppresses the immune response.
    • Mineralocorticoids (e.g., Aldosterone): Regulate balance of water and electrolytes. Acts on renal tubules to reabsorb Na⁺ and water.

Pancreas (Composite Gland)

Acts as both exocrine and endocrine gland. The endocrine pancreas consists of Islets of Langerhans (about 1 to 2 million). Two main types of cells: alpha cells (secrete Glucagon) and beta cells (secrete Insulin).

  • Glucagon: A peptide hormone. Hyperglycemic hormone (increases blood glucose). Stimulates glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis in liver.
  • Insulin: A peptide hormone. Hypoglycemic hormone (decreases blood glucose). Enhances cellular glucose uptake by hepatocytes and adipocytes. Stimulates glycogenesis.
  • Diabetes Mellitus: Prolonged hyperglycemia is associated with this complex disorder which leads to loss of glucose through urine and formation of harmful ketone bodies.

🔬 5. Mechanism of Hormone Action

Hormones produce their effects on target tissues by binding to specific proteins called hormone receptors located in the target tissues only.

  • Membrane-bound Receptors: Hormones which interact with membrane-bound receptors normally do not enter the target cell, but generate second messengers (e.g., cyclic AMP, IP3, Ca⁺⁺) which in turn regulate cellular metabolism. Mostly protein/peptide hormones.
  • Intracellular Receptors: Receptors are inside the cell (mostly nuclear receptors). The hormone-receptor complex interacts directly with the genome to regulate gene expression or chromosome function. Mostly steroid hormones and iodothyronines.
💡 Short Trick: Chemical Nature of Hormones

Steroid Hormones: Anything related to the adrenal cortex or gonads! (Cortisol, testosterone, estradiol, progesterone).
Amino-acid derivatives: Epinephrine.
Peptide/Protein: Insulin, Glucagon, Pituitary hormones, Hypothalamic hormones.
