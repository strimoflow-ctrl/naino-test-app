🫁 1. Respiratory Organs

Mechanisms of breathing vary among different groups of animals depending mainly on their habitats and levels of organisation.

  • Simple diffusion over body surface: Sponges, coelenterates, flatworms.
  • Moist cuticle (skin): Earthworms.
  • Tracheal tubes: Insects.
  • Gills (Branchial respiration): Most aquatic arthropods and molluscs, fishes.
  • Lungs (Pulmonary respiration): Terrestrial forms like reptiles, birds, mammals. (Amphibians like frogs can respire through their moist skin - cutaneous respiration).

Human Respiratory System

Nostrils -> Nasal passage -> Nasal chamber -> Pharynx -> Larynx -> Trachea -> Primary, Secondary & Tertiary bronchi -> Bronchioles -> Alveoli.

  • Larynx: A cartilaginous box which helps in sound production (sound box). During swallowing, glottis is covered by a thin elastic cartilaginous flap called epiglottis to prevent entry of food into larynx.
  • Trachea: A straight tube extending up to the mid-thoracic cavity, which divides at the level of 5th thoracic vertebra into a right and left primary bronchus. Supported by incomplete cartilaginous rings.
  • Lungs: Covered by a double layered pleura, with pleural fluid between them (reduces friction). The part starting with external nostrils up to terminal bronchioles constitutes the conducting part, whereas the alveoli and their ducts form the respiratory/exchange part.

🌬️ 2. Mechanism of Breathing

Breathing involves two stages: inspiration (atmospheric air is drawn in) and expiration (alveolar air is released out).

Inspiration

  • Initiated by the contraction of the diaphragm which increases the volume of thoracic chamber in the antero-posterior axis.
  • Contraction of external inter-costal muscles lifts up the ribs and sternum, increasing the volume in the dorso-ventral axis.
  • The overall increase in the thoracic volume causes a similar increase in pulmonary volume. This decreases intra-pulmonary pressure to less than atmospheric pressure, forcing air into lungs.

Expiration

  • Relaxation of the diaphragm and inter-costal muscles returns the diaphragm and sternum to their normal positions and reduces the thoracic volume.
  • This increases the intra-pulmonary pressure to slightly above atmospheric pressure causing expulsion of air from the lungs.
  • A healthy human breathes 12-16 times/minute. The volume of air involved in breathing movements can be estimated by using a spirometer.

🔄 3. Exchange of Gases

Alveoli are the primary sites of exchange of gases. Exchange of O₂ and CO₂ occurs by simple diffusion mainly based on pressure/concentration gradient.

Fig: Exchange of gases at the alveolus surface

Diffusion Membrane

The diffusion membrane is made up of three major layers: 1. Thin squamous epithelium of alveoli 2. Endothelium of alveolar capillaries 3. Basement substance in between them. Its total thickness is much less than a millimetre.

🩸 4. Transport of Gases

Transport of Oxygen

About 97% of O₂ is transported by RBCs in the blood. Remaining 3% is carried in dissolved state in plasma.

  • O₂ binds with haemoglobin reversibly to form oxyhaemoglobin. Each Hb molecule can carry a maximum of four molecules of O₂.
  • A sigmoid curve is obtained when percentage saturation of Hb with O₂ is plotted against the pO₂ (Oxygen dissociation curve).
  • Factors favoring dissociation (shift to right): Low pO₂, high pCO₂, high H⁺ concentration, high temperature. (These conditions exist in the tissues).
  • Every 100 ml of oxygenated blood can deliver around 5 ml of O₂ to the tissues under normal physiological conditions.

Transport of Carbon Dioxide

CO₂ is carried by Hb as carbamino-haemoglobin (about 20-25%). Transported as bicarbonate (about 70%). Dissolved state in plasma (about 7%).

  • RBCs contain a very high concentration of the enzyme carbonic anhydrase. At tissues, high pCO₂ leads to formation of HCO₃⁻ and H⁺. At alveoli, reaction goes in opposite direction leading to formation of CO₂ and H₂O.
  • Every 100 ml of deoxygenated blood delivers approximately 4 ml of CO₂ to the alveoli.
🚨 Exam Facts: Oxygen Dissociation Curve Shift

In the TISSUES, oxygen needs to be released from Hb. So, conditions like high CO₂ (Bohr effect), high H⁺ (acidic), and high temperature cause the curve to shift to the RIGHT, decreasing Hb's affinity for Oxygen.

🧠 5. Regulation and Disorders

Regulation of Respiration

  • Respiratory Rhythm Centre: Specialised centre in the medulla region of the brain, primarily responsible for this regulation.
  • Pneumotaxic Centre: Located in the pons region, it can moderate the functions of the respiratory rhythm centre (reduces duration of inspiration).
  • Chemosensitive Area: Situated adjacent to rhythm centre, highly sensitive to CO₂ and hydrogen ions. (Role of oxygen in regulation is quite insignificant).

Disorders of Respiratory System

  • Asthma: Difficulty in breathing causing wheezing due to inflammation of bronchi and bronchioles.
  • Emphysema: A chronic disorder in which alveolar walls are damaged due to which respiratory surface is decreased. One of the major causes is cigarette smoking.
  • Occupational Respiratory Disorders: In grinding/stone-breaking industries, dust leads to proliferation of fibrous tissues (fibrosis) causing serious lung damage. Examples: Silicosis, Asbestosis.
🤯 Amazing Info: Lung Capacity of Athletes

While an average person's total lung capacity is about 6 liters, elite swimmers and rowers can have a lung capacity of up to 8 to 12 liters!
