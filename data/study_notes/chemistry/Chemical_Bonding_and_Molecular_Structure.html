🔗 1. Types of Chemical Bonds

Atoms combine to achieve a stable octet (8 electrons in valence shell) and lower energy.

  • Ionic Bond: Complete transfer of one or more electrons from a metal to a non-metal. (e.g., NaCl). Favored by low Ionization Enthalpy of metal and high negative Electron Gain Enthalpy of non-metal.
  • Covalent Bond: Equal sharing of electrons between non-metals. (e.g., H2, Cl2, CH4).
  • Coordinate (Dative) Bond: A special covalent bond where the shared pair of electrons is contributed by only one atom. (e.g., NH4+, O3).
💡 Fajan's Rules (Covalent Character in Ionic Bonds)

An ionic bond acquires covalent character if polarization occurs. High polarizing power and high polarizability favor covalent character.

Covalent character increases with:

  • Small size of cation
  • Large size of anion
  • High charge on cation or anion
  • Pseudo noble gas configuration of cation (e.g., Cu+ is more covalent than Na+)

📐 2. VSEPR Theory (Shapes of Molecules)

Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion theory: The shape of a molecule depends on the number of valence shell electron pairs (bonded or non-bonded) around the central atom. Electron pairs repel each other to attain maximum distance and minimum energy.

Order of Repulsion: Lone Pair - Lone Pair (lp-lp) > Lone Pair - Bond Pair (lp-bp) > Bond Pair - Bond Pair (bp-bp).

🚨 Exam Facts: Lone Pair Distortions
  • Ammonia (NH3): 3 bp + 1 lp → Pyramidal. Bond angle ~ 107°.
  • Water (H2O): 2 bp + 2 lp → Bent / V-shape. Bond angle ~ 104.5°.

⚡ 3. Hybridization

The process of intermixing of atomic orbitals of slightly different energies to produce entirely new set of equivalent orbitals known as hybrid orbitals.

💡 Short Trick: Finding Hybridization

Steric Number (H) = Number of sigma (σ) bonds + Number of lone pairs on central atom.

  • H = 2 → sp (Linear, e.g., BeCl2, CO2)
  • H = 3 → sp2 (Trigonal planar, e.g., BF3, SO2)
  • H = 4 → sp3 (Tetrahedral, e.g., CH4, H2O)
  • H = 5 → sp3d (Trigonal bipyramidal, e.g., PCl5)
  • H = 6 → sp3d2 (Octahedral, e.g., SF6)

🌐 4. Molecular Orbital Theory (MOT)

Atomic orbitals combine to form molecular orbitals (MO). Number of MOs formed = Number of AOs combined.

  • Bonding MO: Lower energy, stable. Formed by addition of wave functions.
  • Anti-bonding MO (*): Higher energy, unstable. Formed by subtraction.
💡 Bond Order

Bond Order = ½ (Nb - Na)

Where Nb = electrons in bonding MOs, Na = electrons in anti-bonding MOs.

Higher bond order → Greater Bond Dissociation Energy → Shorter Bond Length.

If Bond Order is zero or negative, the molecule does not exist (e.g., He2, Ne2).

Magnetic Property: If all electrons are paired in MOs, the molecule is diamagnetic (e.g., N2, F2). If it has unpaired electrons, it is paramagnetic (e.g., O2, B2).

💧 5. Hydrogen Bonding

The electrostatic force of attraction between the covalently bonded hydrogen atom of one molecule and an electronegative atom (F, O, N) of the same or another molecule.

  • Intermolecular H-bond: Formed between two different molecules. Increases boiling point and solubility in water. (e.g., HF, H2O, NH3, p-Nitrophenol).
  • Intramolecular H-bond: Formed within the same molecule. Usually decreases boiling point. (e.g., o-Nitrophenol).
🤯 Amazing Info: Ice floats on water

Due to extensive intermolecular hydrogen bonding, water molecules in ice form a cage-like 3D structure with a lot of empty space. This makes the density of ice less than that of liquid water, causing it to float!
