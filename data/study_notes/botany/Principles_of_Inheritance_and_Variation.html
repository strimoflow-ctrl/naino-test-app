🧬 1. Mendel's Laws of Inheritance

Gregor Mendel, conducted hybridisation experiments on garden peas (Pisum sativum) for seven years (1856-1863) and proposed the laws of inheritance. He was the first to use statistical analysis and mathematical logic to biology problems.

The Seven Contrasting Traits

Stem heightTallDwarf
Flower colourVioletWhite

Seed colourYellowGreen
🤯 Amazing Info: Mendel's Luck

Did you know? Mendel was extremely lucky! All seven traits he studied happened to be located on different chromosomes or were far enough apart that they did not show linkage. If they were linked, his Law of Independent Assortment would have failed!

📊 2. Inheritance of One Gene (Monohybrid Cross)

A cross between two parents differing in only one heritable character.

  • F₁ generation: Always resembled either one of the parents. The trait expressed is Dominant.
  • F₂ generation: Both traits were expressed in the proportion 3:1. The trait that was hidden in F₁ is Recessive.
Fig: Punnett Square showing F2 generation of a monohybrid cross

Test Cross

A cross between the F₁ generation (unknown genotype) and the recessive parent. Used to determine whether a dominant phenotype is homozygous (TT) or heterozygous (Tt).

Mendel's Laws based on Monohybrid Cross

  • Law of Dominance: Characters are controlled by discrete units called factors. In a dissimilar pair of factors one dominates the other.
  • Law of Segregation: The alleles do not show any blending and both characters are recovered in the F₂ generation. Gametes receive only one of the two factors. (This law has NO exceptions).

🚫 3. Exceptions to Mendel's Laws

Incomplete Dominance

F₁ phenotype does not resemble either of the two parents and is in between the two. Example: Flower color in Dog flower (Snapdragon / Antirrhinum). Cross between True breeding Red (RR) and White (rr) gives Pink (Rr) in F₁.
Phenotypic & Genotypic ratio in F₂ is the SAME = 1:2:1.

Co-dominance

F₁ generation resembles BOTH parents. Both alleles express themselves equally. Example: ABO Blood Grouping in humans. IA and IB are totally dominant over i, but when IA and IB are present together, they both express their own types of sugars (Blood group AB).

Multiple Alleles

More than two alleles governing the same character. E.g., ABO blood grouping (controlled by three alleles IA, IB, i). Can only be found in population studies, as one individual only has two alleles.

Pleiotropy

A single gene can exhibit multiple phenotypic expression. Example: The gene causing Phenylketonuria. A mutation in the gene for enzyme phenylalanine hydroxylase also leads to mental retardation and a reduction in hair and skin pigmentation.

⛓️ 4. Linkage and Recombination

T.H. Morgan worked with fruit flies (Drosophila melanogaster) and discovered the basis for the variation that sexual reproduction produced.

  • Linkage: Physical association of genes on a chromosome. Linked genes do not assort independently.
  • Recombination: Generation of non-parental gene combinations due to crossing over.

Morgan found that genes White and Yellow (for eye and body color in Drosophila) were very tightly linked and showed only 1.3% recombination, whereas White and Miniature wing showed 37.2% recombination.

Note: His student Alfred Sturtevant used the frequency of recombination between gene pairs on the same chromosome as a measure of the distance between genes and mapped their position (Genetic mapping).

🧬 5. Genetic Disorders

Mendelian Disorders

Mainly determined by alteration or mutation in a single gene. Can be traced using Pedigree analysis.

  • Haemophilia: Sex-linked recessive. Blood fails to clot. Queen Victoria was a carrier.
  • Sickle-cell anaemia: Autosomal recessive. Substitution of Glutamic acid by Valine at the 6th position of the beta globin chain. Causes RBCs to become sickle-shaped under low oxygen.
  • Phenylketonuria: Autosomal recessive. Lacks enzyme to convert phenylalanine to tyrosine. Causes mental retardation.
  • Thalassemia: Autosomal recessive blood disease. Reduced synthesis of globin chains.
  • Colour Blindness: Sex-linked recessive. Defect in red or green cone of eye.

Chromosomal Disorders

Caused by absence, excess or abnormal arrangement of one or more chromosomes.

  • Down's Syndrome: Trisomy of chromosome 21 (47, XX,+21 or 47, XY,+21). Broad palm with characteristic palm crease, mental retardation.
  • Klinefelter's Syndrome: Presence of an additional copy of X-chromosome (47, XXY). Overall masculine development, but feminine development (gynaecomastia) is expressed. Sterile.
  • Turner's Syndrome: Absence of one of the X chromosomes (45, X0). Females are sterile, ovaries are rudimentary.
💡 Short Trick: Autosomal vs Sex-Linked

Autosomal Recessive: SPA-T (Sickle-cell, Phenylketonuria, Albinism, Thalassemia)
Sex-Linked Recessive: HC (Haemophilia, Colour blindness)
Autosomal Dominant: Myotonic dystrophy (M for Master/Dominant)
