🔬 1. The Tissues

A tissue is a group of cells having a common origin and usually performing a common function. Plant tissues are classified into two main groups: Meristematic and Permanent.

Meristematic Tissues

  • Apical Meristem: Occurs at the tips of roots and shoots. Produces primary tissues. Responsible for increasing the length.
  • Intercalary Meristem: Occurs between mature tissues (e.g., in grasses). They regenerate parts removed by grazing herbivores. Both Apical and Intercalary are Primary Meristems.
  • Lateral Meristem (Secondary Meristem): Occurs in the mature regions of roots and shoots. E.g., fascicular vascular cambium, interfascicular cambium, and cork cambium. Responsible for increasing girth (Secondary growth).

Permanent Tissues

Cells that have lost the ability to divide. Divided into Simple and Complex.

Simple Permanent Tissues (Made of one type of cells)

  • Parenchyma: Forms major bulk. Isodiametric, thin cellulosic walls. Functions: Photosynthesis, storage, secretion.
  • Collenchyma: Found below epidermis in dicots. Thickened at corners due to deposition of cellulose, hemicellulose and pectin. Provides mechanical support to growing parts (young stem, petiole).
  • Sclerenchyma: Long, narrow cells with thick, lignified cell walls. Usually dead. Divided into fibres and sclereids (found in fruit walls of nuts, pulp of fruits like guava, pear, sapota).
🤯 Amazing Info: Why are pears gritty?

Have you noticed the crunchy, sand-like texture when eating a pear or guava? This is entirely due to the presence of Sclereids (Stone Cells), a type of dead, highly thickened sclerenchyma tissue!

🌿 2. Complex Permanent Tissues

Made of more than one type of cells that work together as a unit.

Xylem (Water Conduction)

  • Tracheids: Elongated cells, thick lignified walls, dead. Main water-transporting element in gymnosperms.
  • Vessels: Long cylindrical tube-like structure made of many cells called vessel members. Presence of vessels is a characteristic feature of angiosperms.
  • Xylem Fibres: Highly thickened walls.
  • Xylem Parenchyma: Living and thin-walled. Stores food as starch or fat.

Protoxylem (first formed) and Metaxylem (later formed). Endarch: Protoxylem lies towards center (Stem). Exarch: Protoxylem lies towards periphery (Root).

Phloem (Food Conduction)

  • Sieve Tube Elements: Long, tube-like. Mature sieve tube lacks a nucleus but has a peripheral cytoplasm. Functions controlled by nucleus of companion cell.
  • Companion Cells: Specialized parenchymatous cells closely associated with sieve tubes. Help maintain pressure gradient.
  • Phloem Parenchyma: Absent in most monocots.
  • Phloem Fibres (Bast Fibres): Dead, sclerenchymatous. Used commercially (e.g., jute, flax, hemp).
💡 Short Trick: Endarch vs Exarch

RESE: Root = Exarch, Stem = Endarch.
Remember that roots are under the earth (Exarch - exit towards outside), stems go inside out!

🍁 3. The Tissue System

Based on structure and location, there are three types of tissue systems.

1. Epidermal Tissue System

  • Forms the outermost covering. Comprises epidermal cells, stomata, and epidermal appendages (trichomes and hairs).
  • Stomata: Enclosed by two guard cells. In dicots, guard cells are bean-shaped. In grasses (monocots), they are dumb-bell shaped.
  • Trichomes: Epidermal hairs on the stem. Multicellular. Help in preventing water loss.

2. Ground Tissue System

  • All tissues except epidermis and vascular bundles constitute the ground tissue.
  • Consists of simple tissues like parenchyma, collenchyma, sclerenchyma.
  • In leaves, ground tissue consists of thin-walled chloroplast containing cells and is called mesophyll.

3. Vascular Tissue System

  • Radial: Xylem and phloem are arranged in an alternate manner on different radii (found in Roots).
  • Conjoint: Xylem and phloem are jointly situated along the same radius (found in Stems and Leaves).

🌳 4. Anatomy of Root, Stem and Leaf

Dicot vs Monocot Root

Feature Dicot Root Monocot Root
Vascular Bundles Diarch to Hexarch (2 to 6 xylem bundles) Polyarch (more than 6 xylem bundles)
Pith Small or inconspicuous Large and well developed
Secondary Growth Occurs (cambium develops) Does not occur

Dicot vs Monocot Stem

Feature Dicot Stem Monocot Stem
Vascular Bundles Arranged in a ring, open (cambium present) Scattered, closed (cambium absent)
Bundle Sheath Absent Present (Sclerenchymatous)
Pith Present (central parenchyma) Absent (ground tissue is undifferentiated)
🚨 Exam Facts: Casparian Strips

In the endodermis of roots, the tangential and radial walls have a deposition of water-impermeable, waxy material called suberin in the form of Casparian strips. This ensures that water moving through the apoplast is forced to cross the cell membrane into the symplast before entering the xylem!

🪵 5. Secondary Growth

The increase in girth is called secondary growth. It occurs due to the activity of two lateral meristems: Vascular Cambium and Cork Cambium. (Mostly in Dicots & Gymnosperms).

Vascular Cambium

  • Cuts off cells on both sides. Cells cut towards the inner side mature into secondary xylem (wood), and towards the outside into secondary phloem.
  • Spring wood (Early wood): Cambium is very active, produces a large number of xylem elements having vessels with wider cavities. Lighter in color, lower density.
  • Autumn wood (Late wood): Cambium is less active, narrow vessels. Darker in color, higher density.
  • One ring of spring wood and one ring of autumn wood constitute an Annual Ring (used to estimate age of trees - Dendrochronology).

Heartwood and Sapwood

  • Heartwood: Central, dark-colored wood. Dead, highly lignified, filled with tannins, resins, oils. Highly durable and resistant to microbes. Does NOT conduct water.
  • Sapwood: Peripheral, lighter-colored wood. Living and conducts water and minerals.

Cork Cambium (Phellogen)

As the stem continues to increase in girth, outer cortical and epidermis layers get broken. Phellogen develops in the cortex. It cuts off cells on both sides:

  • Outer cells differentiate into Cork (Phellem). Impervious to water due to suberin.
  • Inner cells differentiate into Secondary Cortex (Phelloderm).

Phellem + Phellogen + Phelloderm = Periderm.

🤯 Amazing Info: Bark vs Wood

Did you know? Everything outside the vascular cambium is technically called Bark. This means Bark includes the secondary phloem (which carries food)! If you completely strip a ring of bark from a tree trunk, the tree will die because the roots starve without the phloem!
