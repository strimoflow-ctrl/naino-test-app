🌍 1. Ecosystem Structure and Function

An ecosystem can be visualized as a functional unit of nature, where living organisms interact among themselves and also with the surrounding physical environment. Examples: Forest, Grassland, Pond, Crop field, Aquarium.

Key Components

Interaction of biotic and abiotic components results in a physical structure characteristic for each type of ecosystem. The four basic functional components are:

  1. Productivity: Rate of biomass production.
    • Gross Primary Productivity (GPP): Rate of production of organic matter during photosynthesis.
    • Net Primary Productivity (NPP): GPP minus respiration losses (R). NPP = GPP - R. This is the available biomass for consumption to heterotrophs.
    • Secondary Productivity: Rate of formation of new organic matter by consumers.
  2. Decomposition: Breakdown of complex organic matter into inorganic substances (CO₂, water, nutrients) by decomposers. Steps involved: Fragmentation, Leaching, Catabolism, Humification, and Mineralisation.
  3. Energy Flow: Unidirectional flow of energy from sun to producers to consumers.
  4. Nutrient Cycling: Movement of nutrient elements through various components of an ecosystem.
🚨 Exam Facts: Global Productivity

The annual net primary productivity of the whole biosphere is approximately 170 billion tons (dry weight) of organic matter. Of this, despite occupying about 70% of the surface, the productivity of the oceans is only 55 billion tons!

⚡ 2. Energy Flow

Except for the deep sea hydro-thermal ecosystem, sun is the only source of energy for all ecosystems on Earth.

  • Of the incident solar radiation, less than 50% is Photosynthetically Active Radiation (PAR).
  • Plants capture only 2-10% of the PAR, and this small amount of energy sustains the entire living world.
  • Energy flow is strictly unidirectional and follows the laws of thermodynamics.

Food Chains

  • Grazing Food Chain (GFC): Begins with producers (plants) -> Herbivores -> Carnivores. Major conduit of energy flow in AQUATIC ecosystems.
  • Detritus Food Chain (DFC): Begins with dead organic matter (detritus) -> Decomposers (saprotrophs like fungi and bacteria). Major conduit of energy flow in TERRESTRIAL ecosystems.

Food chains become interconnected to form a complex network called a Food Web.

🔺 3. Ecological Pyramids

The relationship between different trophic levels can be expressed in terms of number, biomass, or energy. The base of each pyramid represents the producers or the first trophic level.

  • Pyramid of Number: Usually upright. Exceptions: Tree ecosystem (inverted - 1 tree supports many insects).
  • Pyramid of Biomass: Usually upright. Exception: Sea/Ocean ecosystem is inverted because the biomass of fishes far exceeds that of phytoplankton.
  • Pyramid of Energy: ALWAYS upright, can never be inverted. Because when energy flows from one trophic level to the next, some energy is always lost as heat at each step (Lindeman's 10% law).

🌲 4. Ecological Succession

The gradual and fairly predictable change in the species composition of a given area is called ecological succession.

  • Pioneer Species: The species that invade a bare area.
  • Climax Community: The final community that is in near equilibrium with the environment. Remains stable as long as the environment remains unchanged.
  • Primary Succession: Begins in areas where no living organisms ever existed (e.g., bare rock, newly cooled lava). Extremely slow (takes thousands of years).
  • Secondary Succession: Begins in areas where natural biotic communities have been destroyed (e.g., abandoned farm lands, burned forests). Faster than primary succession.

Succession on Rocks (Xerarch)

Pioneer species: Lichens (secrete acids to dissolve rock) -> Bryophytes -> Herbaceous plants -> Shrubs -> Forest (Climax).

Succession in Water (Hydrarch)

Pioneer species: Phytoplankton -> Free-floating angiosperms -> Rooted hydrophytes -> Sedges -> Grasses -> Trees (Climax).

Note: Both Hydrarch and Xerarch successions lead to medium water conditions (Mesic climax community).

🔄 5. Nutrient Cycling (Biogeochemical Cycles)

The movement of nutrient elements through the various components of an ecosystem. Nutrient is never lost from the ecosystems, they are recycled time and again indefinitely.

Gaseous Cycles (Reservoir in Atmosphere/Hydrosphere)

  • Carbon Cycle: 71% of carbon is dissolved in oceans. Fossil fuels also represent a reservoir. Carbon cycling occurs through atmosphere, ocean, and through living/dead organisms. Human activities (deforestation, burning fossil fuels) have significantly increased the rate of release of CO₂ into the atmosphere.

Sedimentary Cycles (Reservoir in Earth's Crust)

  • Phosphorus Cycle: Phosphorus is a major constituent of biological membranes, nucleic acids, and cellular energy transfer systems. The natural reservoir is Rock. When rocks weather, minute amounts dissolve in soil solution and are absorbed by roots of plants.

Difference: Atmospheric inputs of phosphorus through rainfall are much smaller than carbon inputs, and gaseous exchanges of phosphorus between organism and environment are negligible.
