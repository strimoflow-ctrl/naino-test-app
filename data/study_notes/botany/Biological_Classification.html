Biological Classification

📚 1. History of Classification

Since the dawn of civilisation, there have been many attempts to classify living organisms. It was done instinctively not using criteria that were scientific but borne out of a need to use organisms for our own use – for food, shelter and clothing.

Aristotle's Classification

  • Earliest to attempt a more scientific basis for classification.
  • Used simple morphological characters to classify plants into trees, shrubs and herbs.
  • Divided animals into two groups: those which had red blood and those that did not.

Two Kingdom Classification (Linnaeus)

  • Developed by Carolus Linnaeus.
  • Kingdoms: Plantae and Animalia.
  • Drawbacks: Did not distinguish between eukaryotes and prokaryotes, unicellular and multicellular organisms, and photosynthetic (green algae) and non-photosynthetic (fungi) organisms.

Five Kingdom Classification (R.H. Whittaker - 1969)

  • Kingdoms: Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae, Animalia.
  • Main criteria used: Cell structure, body organisation, mode of nutrition, reproduction, and phylogenetic relationships.
🚨 NEET HIGH-YIELD: Chlamydomonas & Amoeba

In earlier systems (like Linnaeus), Chlamydomonas and Spirogyra were placed under Algae in Plants. Amoeba and Paramecium were under Animal kingdom. Whittaker's 5-Kingdom system placed all unicellular eukaryotes (including Chlamydomonas, Paramecium, Amoeba) together in Kingdom Protista.

🦠 2. Kingdom Monera

Bacteria are the sole members of the Kingdom Monera. They are the most abundant micro-organisms. Hundreds of bacteria are present in a handful of soil.

Shapes of Bacteria

  • Coccus: Spherical
  • Bacillus: Rod-shaped
  • Vibrio: Comma-shaped
  • Spirillum: Spiral-shaped

Archaebacteria

They are special since they live in some of the most harsh habitats:

  • Halophiles: Extreme salty areas.
  • Thermoacidophiles: Hot springs.
  • Methanogens: Marshy areas and gut of ruminants (cows, buffaloes). Responsible for biogas (methane) production.

They differ from other bacteria in having a different cell wall structure which helps them survive extreme conditions.

Eubacteria (True Bacteria)

Characterised by the presence of a rigid cell wall, and if motile, a flagellum.

  • Cyanobacteria (Blue-green algae): Photosynthetic autotrophs. Have chlorophyll a similar to green plants. Form blooms in polluted water. Have specialized cells called Heterocysts for nitrogen fixation (e.g., Nostoc, Anabaena).
  • Chemosynthetic autotrophs: Oxidise inorganic substances like nitrates, nitrites, ammonia and use released energy for ATP production. Help in recycling N, P, Fe, S.
  • Heterotrophic bacteria: Most abundant in nature. Mostly decomposers. Helpful in making curd, antibiotics, fixing nitrogen in legume roots. Some are pathogens (Cholera, Typhoid, Tetanus, Citrus canker).
📘 Definition: Mycoplasma

Organisms that completely lack a cell wall. They are the smallest living cells known and can survive without oxygen. Many are pathogenic in animals and plants.

🌊 3. Kingdom Protista

All single-celled eukaryotes are placed under Protista, but the boundaries of this kingdom are not well defined. Primarily aquatic.

Chrysophytes

  • Includes diatoms and golden algae (desmids).
  • Found in fresh water and marine environments.
  • Diatoms: Cell walls form two thin overlapping shells (like a soap box) embedded with silica (indestructible). Accumulation over billions of years forms Diatomaceous earth (used in polishing, filtration). They are the chief producers in the oceans.

Dinoflagellates

  • Mostly marine and photosynthetic. Appear yellow, green, brown, blue, or red.
  • Cell wall has stiff cellulose plates on outer surface.
  • Most have two flagella (one longitudinal, one transverse).
  • Red Tides: Caused by rapid multiplication of red dinoflagellates (e.g., Gonyaulax). Release toxins that kill marine fishes.

Euglenoids

  • Mostly fresh water organisms found in stagnant water.
  • Instead of cell wall, they have a protein-rich layer called pellicle which makes body flexible.
  • Mixotrophic: Photosynthetic in sunlight, heterotrophic when deprived of sunlight.

Slime Moulds

  • Saprophytic protists.
  • Under suitable conditions, form an aggregation called plasmodium.
  • During unfavorable conditions, plasmodium differentiates and forms fruiting bodies bearing spores. Spores have true walls and are extremely resistant.

Protozoans

  • All are heterotrophs (predators or parasites).
  • Amoeboid: Move and capture prey using pseudopodia. Marine forms have silica shells. (e.g., Amoeba, Entamoeba).
  • Flagellated: Have flagella. Parasitic forms cause diseases like sleeping sickness (e.g., Trypanosoma).
  • Ciliated: Have thousands of cilia. Have a gullet that opens outside. (e.g., Paramecium).
  • Sporozoans: Have an infectious spore-like stage in life cycle. (e.g., Plasmodium - malarial parasite).

🍄 4. Kingdom Fungi

Heterotrophic organisms showing great diversity in morphology and habitat. Cell wall composed of chitin and polysaccharides. Most are saprophytes.

Structure

Except yeast (unicellular), fungi are filamentous. Their bodies consist of long, slender thread-like structures called hyphae. The network of hyphae is called mycelium. Some are continuous tubes filled with multinucleated cytoplasm (coenocytic hyphae), others have septa/cross-walls.

🦠 5. Viruses, Viroids, Prions and Lichens

Whittaker's five kingdom system did not include some acellular organisms like viruses and viroids, and lichens.

Viruses

  • Non-cellular organisms that have an inert crystalline structure outside living cell.
  • Once they infect a cell, they take over the host machinery to replicate.
  • Name virus (venom/poison) was given by Pasteur/D.J. Ivanowsky (1892).
  • W.M. Stanley (1935) showed viruses could be crystallized (crystals are largely proteins).
  • Contain genetic material (either RNA or DNA, never both).
  • Protein coat is called capsid made of small subunits called capsomeres, which protects the nucleic acid.

Viroids

  • Discovered by T.O. Diener (1971).
  • Smaller than viruses. Cause potato spindle tuber disease.
  • Found to be free RNA; it lacked the protein coat found in viruses.
  • RNA of viroid was of low molecular weight.

Prions

  • Abnormally folded proteins that cause certain infectious neurological diseases.
  • Similar in size to viruses.
  • Cause BSE (Bovine spongiform encephalopathy) commonly called mad cow disease in cattle, and its analogous variant Cr-Jacob disease (CJD) in humans.

Lichens

  • Symbiotic association between algae (phycobiont) and fungi (mycobiont).
  • Algae prepare food; fungi provide shelter and absorb mineral nutrients/water.
  • Very good pollution indicators (they do not grow in polluted areas, especially SO₂ pollution).
