🌞 1. Introduction and Early Experiments

Photosynthesis is a physico-chemical process by which green plants use light energy to drive the synthesis of organic compounds. It is the primary source of all food on earth and is responsible for the release of oxygen.

Historic Experiments

  • Joseph Priestley (1770): Bell jar, mouse, and mint plant experiment. Discovered that plants restore the air whatever breathing animals and burning candles remove. (Discovered Oxygen in 1774).
  • Jan Ingenhousz: Showed that sunlight is essential to the plant process that somehow purifies the air.
  • Julius von Sachs (1854): Provided evidence for production of glucose when plants grow. Glucose is usually stored as starch.
  • T.W. Engelmann: Using a prism, split light into spectral components and illuminated a green alga (Cladophora). Detected sites of O₂ evolution using aerobic bacteria. Described the first action spectrum.
  • Cornelius van Niel: Demonstrated that photosynthesis is essentially a light-dependent reaction in which hydrogen from a suitable oxidisable compound reduces carbon dioxide to carbohydrates. Proved O₂ comes from H₂O, not CO₂.
🤯 Amazing Info: The Action Spectrum

Did you know? Engelmann used aerobic bacteria to detect where oxygen was being produced! The bacteria accumulated mostly in the blue and red light regions, perfectly matching the absorption spectrum of Chlorophyll a and b!

🍃 2. Site of Photosynthesis

Takes place in the green leaves of plants, specifically in the chloroplasts of the mesophyll cells.

  • Membrane system (Grana & Stroma Lamellae): Responsible for trapping the light energy and also for the synthesis of ATP and NADPH. (Light Reactions).
  • Stroma: Enzymatic reactions incorporate CO₂ into the plant leading to the synthesis of sugar, which in turn forms starch. (Dark Reactions / Calvin Cycle).

Photosynthetic Pigments

Separated by paper chromatography:

  • Chlorophyll a: Bright or blue green in chromatogram. Chief pigment!
  • Chlorophyll b: Yellow green.
  • Xanthophylls: Yellow.
  • Carotenoids: Yellow to yellow-orange.

Chlorophyll a acts as the reaction center. The rest are accessory pigments forming the Light Harvesting Complex (LHC) / Antennae.

⚡ 3. The Light Reaction (Z-Scheme)

Light reactions or the 'Photochemical' phase include light absorption, water splitting, oxygen release, and formation of high-energy chemical intermediates (ATP and NADPH).

Fig: The Z-Scheme of Non-Cyclic Photophosphorylation

Photosystems (PS)

  • Photosystem I (PS I): Reaction center chlorophyll a has an absorption peak at 700 nm, hence called P700.
  • Photosystem II (PS II): Absorption maxima at 680 nm, called P680.

Types of Photophosphorylation

  • Non-Cyclic: Involves both PS I and PS II. Electrons do not return. Produces both ATP and NADPH. Occurs in grana lamellae.
  • Cyclic: Involves only PS I. Electrons circulate within the photosystem. Produces ONLY ATP (no NADPH, no O₂ release). Occurs in stroma lamellae (because it lacks PS II and NADP reductase).
🚨 Exam Facts: Chemiosmotic Hypothesis

Proposed by Peter Mitchell. ATP synthesis is linked to development of a proton gradient across the thylakoid membrane. Protons accumulate inside the thylakoid lumen. When they flow back to the stroma through ATP synthase (CF0-CF1 particle), the energy generated drives ATP formation!

🔄 4. Dark Reaction (Calvin Cycle / C3 Pathway)

Uses ATP and NADPH to drive the synthesis of sugars (CO₂ fixation). Occurs in the stroma. Discovered by Melvin Calvin using radioactive ¹⁴C in algal photosynthesis.

Primary CO₂ acceptor: RuBP (Ribulose 1,5-bisphosphate), a 5-carbon ketose sugar.
First stable product: 3-PGA (3-phosphoglyceric acid), a 3-carbon compound.

Three Stages of Calvin Cycle:

  1. Carboxylation: Most crucial step. CO₂ is utilized for carboxylation of RuBP, catalyzed by the enzyme RuBisCO.
  2. Reduction: Series of reactions leading to glucose formation. Requires 2 ATP and 2 NADPH per CO₂ fixed.
  3. Regeneration: Regeneration of RuBP is crucial for cycle continuation. Requires 1 ATP for phosphorylation.
💡 Short Trick: Calvin Cycle Requirements

For 1 molecule of Glucose (which has 6 Carbons), the cycle must run 6 times!
Total requirement: 18 ATP and 12 NADPH.

🌵 5. C4 Pathway & Photorespiration

C4 Pathway (Hatch & Slack Pathway)

Occurs in plants adapted to dry tropical regions (e.g., Maize, Sorghum). First stable product is a 4-carbon compound, Oxaloacetic Acid (OAA).

  • Show a special leaf anatomy called Kranz anatomy. Mesophyll cells are clustered around bundle-sheath cells.
  • Bundle sheath cells have a large number of chloroplasts, thick walls impervious to gas exchange, and NO intercellular spaces.
  • Primary CO₂ acceptor is PEP (Phosphoenol pyruvate, 3-Carbon) present in mesophyll cells. Enzyme is PEPcase. Mesophyll cells lack RuBisCO.
  • RuBisCO is present in bundle sheath cells where the Calvin cycle ultimately takes place.

Photorespiration

RuBisCO is the most abundant enzyme in the world. Its active site can bind to both CO₂ and O₂. In C3 plants, at high O₂ or high temp, RuBisCO binds to O₂, breaking RuBP into 1 molecule of phosphoglycerate and phosphoglycolate. No sugars or ATP are formed; rather ATP is consumed! This is a wasteful process.

C4 plants lack photorespiration because they have a mechanism that increases CO₂ concentration at the enzyme site in bundle sheath cells.
