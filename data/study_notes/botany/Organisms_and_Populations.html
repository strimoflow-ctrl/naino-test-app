🌍 1. Organism and Its Environment

Ecology is basically concerned with four levels of biological organisation – organisms, populations, communities, and biomes.

Regional and local variations within each biome lead to the formation of a wide variety of habitats. Major biomes of India: Tropical rain forest, Deciduous forest, Desert, Sea coast.

Major Abiotic Factors

  • Temperature: Most ecologically relevant environmental factor. Ranges from subzero levels in polar areas to >50°C in tropical deserts. Affects the kinetics of enzymes. Eurythermal (tolerate wide range), Stenothermal (restricted to narrow range).
  • Water: Life on earth originated in water and is unsustainable without it. For aquatic organisms, pH and salinity are very important. Euryhaline (tolerate wide range of salinities), Stenohaline (restricted to narrow range).
  • Light: Required by plants for photosynthesis. Important for timing of foraging, reproductive and migratory activities in animals. In deep ocean (>500m), it's completely dark.
  • Soil: Nature and properties vary. Determined by weathering, transport, and topography. Affects vegetation type, which in turn dictates the type of animals supported.
🤯 Amazing Info: Deep Sea Hydrothermal Vents

Did you know? In deep-sea hydrothermal vents where the temperature exceeds 100°C, certain archaebacteria flourish! At this depth, there is no sunlight, so they use chemosynthesis instead of photosynthesis to make their food.

🛡️ 2. Responses to Abiotic Factors

Organisms try to maintain the constancy of its internal environment (homeostasis) despite varying external environmental conditions.

  • Regulate: Some organisms maintain homeostasis by physiological (sometimes behavioral) means to ensure constant body temperature (thermoregulation) and constant osmotic concentration (osmoregulation). All birds and mammals.
  • Conform: An overwhelming majority (99%) of animals and nearly all plants cannot maintain a constant internal environment. Their body temperature changes with the ambient temperature.
  • Migrate: The organism can move away temporarily from the stressful habitat to a more hospitable area and return when stressful period is over. (E.g., Siberian cranes coming to Keoladeo National Park, Bharatpur in winter).
  • Suspend: In bacteria, fungi and lower plants, various kinds of thick-walled spores are formed. In higher plants, seeds and vegetative reproductive structures serve as means to tide over periods of stress. Bears go into hibernation during winter. Snails and fish go into aestivation to avoid summer-related problems. Many zooplankton enter diapause, a stage of suspended development.

🌵 3. Adaptations

Adaptation is any attribute of the organism (morphological, physiological, behavioral) that enables the organism to survive and reproduce in its habitat.

Examples of Adaptations

  • Kangaroo Rat (North American Desert): Meets all its water requirements through internal fat oxidation (water is a byproduct). Concentrates its urine so minimal volume of water is used.
  • Desert Plants: Thick cuticle, sunken stomata. Many have CAM pathway (stomata open at night). Opuntia has leaves reduced to spines, and photosynthetic function is taken over by flattened stems.
  • Allen's Rule: Mammals from colder climates generally have shorter ears and limbs to minimize heat loss.
  • Altitude Sickness: At high altitudes (>3500m), low atmospheric pressure causes nausea, fatigue, heart palpitations. Body acclimates by increasing RBC production, decreasing binding affinity of hemoglobin, and increasing breathing rate.
  • Desert Lizards: Behavioral adaptation. They bask in the sun and absorb heat when body temperature drops below comfort zone, but move into shade when ambient temperature starts increasing.
🚨 Exam Facts: Allen's Rule

Remember ALLEN's Rule = "Short appendages". Colder regions = Shorter ears & limbs to reduce surface area for heat loss!

📈 4. Populations

A population is a group of individuals of the same species living in a well-defined geographical area, sharing/competing for resources, and potentially interbreeding.

Population Attributes

  • Birth rates and Death rates: Expressed as per capita births and deaths.
  • Sex Ratio: Ratio of males to females (e.g., 60% females and 40% males).
  • Age Distribution: Plotting the age distribution for a population forms an Age Pyramid (Expanding, Stable, or Declining).
  • Population Density (N): Number of individuals per unit area. Can be measured by relative density (tiger pug marks and fecal pellets).

Population Growth

Depends on four basic processes: Natality (+), Mortality (-), Immigration (+), Emigration (-).

  • Exponential Growth: When resources are unlimited. The equation is dN/dt = rN. The integral form is Nt = N0ert. Gives a J-shaped curve.
  • Logistic Growth: When resources are limited, leading to competition. Gives a sigmoid (S-shaped) curve. Also called Verhulst-Pearl Logistic Growth. Equation is dN/dt = rN(K-N/K), where K is the carrying capacity.

🤝 5. Population Interactions

No species can live in isolation. In nature, plants, animals and microbes interact in various ways to form a biological community.

Competition--Flamingos and resident fishes in S. American lakes competing for zooplankton.

Gause's Competitive Exclusion Principle

Two closely related species competing for the same resources cannot co-exist indefinitely and the competitively inferior one will be eliminated eventually (if resources are limiting). However, species often evolve mechanisms like Resource Partitioning to co-exist.

💡 Short Trick: Interactions signs

Mutualism: Win-Win (+/+)
Competition: Lose-Lose (-/-)
Predation/Parasitism: Win-Lose (+/-)
Commensalism: Win-Neutral (+/0)
Amensalism: Lose-Neutral (-/0)
