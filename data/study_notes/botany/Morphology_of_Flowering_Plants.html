🌱 1. The Root

In majority of dicotyledonous plants, the direct elongation of the radicle leads to the formation of primary root. It bears lateral roots of several orders that are referred to as secondary, tertiary, etc. roots. The primary root and its branches constitute the tap root system, e.g., mustard plant.

In monocotyledonous plants, the primary root is short-lived and is replaced by a large number of roots. These roots originate from the base of the stem and constitute the fibrous root system, e.g., wheat plant.

In some plants, like grass, Monstera and the banyan tree, roots arise from parts of the plant other than the radicle and are called adventitious roots.

Regions of the Root

  • Root Cap: Thimble-like structure covering the apex. Protects the tender apex.
  • Region of Meristematic Activity: Few millimeters above root cap. Cells divide repeatedly, are small, thin-walled with dense protoplasm.
  • Region of Elongation: Cells undergo rapid elongation and enlargement, responsible for the growth of the root in length.
  • Region of Maturation: Cells differentiate and mature. Epidermal cells from this region form very fine, delicate, thread-like structures called root hairs (they absorb water and minerals).

Modifications of Root

  • For Food Storage: Tap roots of carrot, turnip and adventitious roots of sweet potato.
  • For Support: Prop roots (hanging structures in banyan tree) and Stilt roots (coming out of lower nodes of stem in maize and sugarcane).
  • For Respiration: Pneumatophores (growing vertically upwards to get oxygen in Rhizophora growing in swampy areas).

🌿 2. The Stem

The stem is the ascending part of the axis developing from the plumule of the embryo of a germinating seed. The stem bears nodes and internodes.

Modifications of Stem

  • Underground Stems: Modified to store food and act as organs of perennation. Examples: Potato, ginger, turmeric, zaminkand, Colocasia.
  • Stem Tendrils: Slender and spirally coiled structures developing from axillary buds, help in climbing. Examples: Gourds (cucumber, pumpkins, watermelon) and grapevines.
  • Thorns: Axillary buds modified into woody, straight and pointed thorns to protect plants from browsing animals. Examples: Citrus, Bougainvillea.
  • Phylloclade: Stem flattened (Opuntia) or fleshy cylindrical (Euphorbia) containing chlorophyll and carrying out photosynthesis.
  • Runners, Stolons, Offsets, Suckers: Used for vegetative propagation (e.g., grass, strawberry, mint, jasmine, Pistia, Eichhornia, banana, pineapple, chrysanthemum).

🍃 3. The Leaf

The leaf is a lateral, generally flattened structure borne on the stem. It develops at the node and bears a bud in its axil. Leaves originate from shoot apical meristems and are arranged in an acropetal order.

Leaf Parts

A typical leaf consists of three main parts: leaf base, petiole, and lamina (leaf blade).

  • In monocots, the leaf base expands into a sheath covering the stem partially or wholly.
  • In some leguminous plants, the leaf base may become swollen, which is called the pulvinus.

Venation

The arrangement of veins and the veinlets in the lamina of leaf is termed as venation.

  • Reticulate: Veinlets form a network. Found in dicotyledons.
  • Parallel: Veins run parallel to each other. Found in monocotyledons.

Phyllotaxy

It is the pattern of arrangement of leaves on the stem or branch.

  • Alternate: Single leaf arises at each node in alternate manner. (e.g., China rose, mustard, sunflower).
  • Opposite: A pair of leaves arise at each node and lie opposite to each other. (e.g., Calotropis, guava).
  • Whorled: More than two leaves arise at a node and form a whorl. (e.g., Alstonia).

🌸 4. The Flower and Inflorescence

The arrangement of flowers on the floral axis is termed as inflorescence.

  • Racemose: Main axis continues to grow, flowers are borne laterally in an acropetal succession.
  • Cymose: Main axis terminates in a flower, hence is limited in growth. Flowers are borne in a basipetal order.

Aestivation

The mode of arrangement of sepals or petals in floral bud with respect to the other members of the same whorl.

  • Valvate: Margins just touch one another, without overlapping (e.g., Calotropis).
  • Twisted: One margin overlaps that of the next one (e.g., China rose, lady's finger, cotton).
  • Imbricate: Margins overlap one another but not in any particular direction (e.g., Cassia, gulmohur).
  • Vexillary (Papilionaceous): Five petals; largest (standard) overlaps two lateral petals (wings), which in turn overlap the two smallest anterior petals (keel). Found in pea and bean flowers.

Placentation

The arrangement of ovules within the ovary is known as placentation.

  • Marginal: Placenta forms a ridge along ventral suture, ovules are borne on this ridge forming two rows (e.g., pea).
  • Axile: Placenta is axial and ovules are attached to it in a multilocular ovary (e.g., China rose, tomato, lemon).
  • Parietal: Ovules develop on the inner wall of the ovary. Ovary is one-chambered but becomes two-chambered due to formation of false septum (e.g., mustard, Argemone).
  • Free Central: Ovules are borne on central axis and septa are absent (e.g., Dianthus, Primrose).
  • Basal: Placenta develops at the base of ovary and a single ovule is attached to it (e.g., sunflower, marigold).

🍎 5. The Fruit and Seed

The fruit is a characteristic feature of the flowering plants. It is a mature or ripened ovary, developed after fertilization. If a fruit is formed without fertilization of the ovary, it is called a parthenocarpic fruit.

The Seed

The ovules after fertilization develop into seeds.

  • Structure of Dicot Seed: Outermost covering is seed coat (outer testa, inner tegmen). The hilum is a scar through which developing seeds were attached to the fruit. Above hilum is a small pore called micropyle. Within the seed coat is the embryo, consisting of an embryonal axis and two cotyledons. At the two ends of embryonal axis are radicle and plumule.
  • Structure of Monocot Seed: Generally endospermic (e.g., cereals like maize). Seed coat is membranous and generally fused with fruit wall. The endosperm is bulky and stores food. The outer covering of endosperm separates the embryo by a proteinous layer called aleurone layer. The embryo is small and situated in a groove at one end. It consists of one large and shield-shaped cotyledon known as scutellum and a short axis with a plumule and a radicle. Plumule is enclosed in a sheath called coleoptile and radicle in coleorhiza.
