🧀 1. Microbes in Household Products

Microbes are a major component of biological systems. Not all microbes are pathogenic; many are highly useful to humans.

  • Lactobacillus (LAB): Converts milk to curd. LAB produces acids that coagulate and partially digest milk proteins. It also improves nutritional quality by increasing Vitamin B12. In the stomach, LAB checks disease-causing microbes.
  • Saccharomyces cerevisiae: The baker's yeast. Used for making bread (dough ferments and rises due to CO₂ production).
  • Toddy: Traditional drink of Southern India made by fermenting sap from palms.
  • Cheese: Different varieties are known by characteristic texture, flavor, and taste.
    - Swiss cheese: Large holes due to production of large amount of CO₂ by bacterium Propionibacterium sharmanii.
    - Roquefort cheese: Ripened by growing a specific fungus on them to give a particular flavor.

🏭 2. Microbes in Industrial Products

For industrial production, microbes are grown in very large vessels called fermentors.

Fermented Beverages

Saccharomyces cerevisiae (Brewer's yeast) is used for fermenting malted cereals and fruit juices to produce ethanol.

  • Without distillation: Wine and beer.
  • With distillation: Whisky, brandy, rum.

Antibiotics

Chemical substances produced by some microbes that can kill or retard the growth of other (disease-causing) microbes.

  • Penicillin: First antibiotic discovered by Alexander Fleming from mould Penicillium notatum. Its full potential was established later by Ernest Chain and Howard Florey (they all shared the Nobel Prize in 1945). Used extensively to treat American soldiers in WWII.

🚨 Exam Facts: Important Bioactive Molecules
  • Streptokinase: Produced by Streptococcus (modified by genetic engineering). Used as a 'clot buster' for removing clots from blood vessels of patients suffering myocardial infarction.
  • Cyclosporin A: Produced by fungus Trichoderma polysporum. Used as an immunosuppressive agent in organ-transplant patients.
  • Statins: Produced by yeast Monascus purpureus. Act as blood-cholesterol lowering agents by competitively inhibiting the enzyme responsible for cholesterol synthesis.

💧 3. Microbes in Sewage Treatment

Municipal waste-water (sewage) contains large amounts of organic matter and microbes. Treated in Sewage Treatment Plants (STPs) to make it less polluting before disposal into rivers.

Primary Treatment (Physical)

Physical removal of particles through filtration and sedimentation. Sequential filtration removes floating debris. Sedimentation removes soil/small pebbles (forms primary sludge). The supernatant forms the effluent.

Secondary Treatment (Biological)

Primary effluent is passed into large aeration tanks where it is constantly agitated and air is pumped into it. This allows vigorous growth of useful aerobic microbes into flocs (masses of bacteria associated with fungal filaments to form mesh-like structures).

  • These microbes consume the major part of the organic matter, significantly reducing the BOD (Biochemical Oxygen Demand). Lower BOD means lower polluting potential.
  • Once BOD is reduced, effluent passes to settling tank where flocs sediment. This sediment is called activated sludge.
  • A small part of activated sludge is pumped back to the aeration tank as inoculum. The remaining major part is pumped into anaerobic sludge digesters. Here, anaerobic bacteria digest the bacteria and fungi in the sludge, producing a mixture of gases: methane, H₂S, and CO₂ (Biogas).

The Ministry of Environment and Forests initiated the Ganga Action Plan and Yamuna Action Plan to build a large number of STPs.

🔥 4. Microbes in Biogas Production

Biogas is a mixture of gases (predominantly methane) produced by microbial activity. Certain bacteria that grow anaerobically on cellulosic material produce large amounts of methane along with CO₂ and H₂. These are collectively called methanogens (e.g., Methanobacterium).

  • These bacteria are commonly found in the anaerobic sludge during sewage treatment.
  • They are also present in the rumen (a part of stomach) of cattle, helping in breakdown of cellulose. Therefore, cattle dung (gobar) is rich in these bacteria.
  • Biogas plant consists of a concrete tank (10-15 feet deep) in which bio-wastes are collected and a slurry of dung is fed. A floating cover is placed over it. The biogas produced is supplied to nearby houses through a pipe. Spent slurry is removed to be used as fertilizer.
🤯 Amazing Info: IARI and KVIC

The technology of biogas production was developed in India mainly due to the efforts of the Indian Agricultural Research Institute (IARI) and Khadi and Village Industries Commission (KVIC)!

🛡️ 5. Microbes as Biocontrol Agents & Biofertilisers

Biocontrol Agents

Use of biological methods for controlling plant diseases and pests (reduces dependence on toxic chemicals and pesticides).

  • Ladybird & Dragonflies: Useful to get rid of aphids and mosquitoes, respectively.
  • Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt): Bacteria whose spores produce a toxin that kills caterpillars of butterflies/moths in their gut. Used in Bt-Cotton.
  • Trichoderma: Free-living fungi found in root ecosystems. Effective biocontrol agents of several plant pathogens.
  • Baculoviruses (Nucleopolyhedrovirus): Pathogens that attack insects and other arthropods. Excellent for species-specific, narrow spectrum insecticidal applications (Integrated Pest Management - IPM).

Biofertilisers

Organisms that enrich the nutrient quality of the soil.

  • Rhizobium: Symbiotic bacteria in root nodules of leguminous plants. Fix atmospheric nitrogen.
  • Azospirillum and Azotobacter: Free-living bacteria in the soil that fix nitrogen.
  • Mycorrhiza: Fungi (many members of genus Glomus) form symbiotic associations with roots of higher plants. The fungal hyphae absorb phosphorus from soil and pass it to the plant.
  • Cyanobacteria: Autotrophic microbes (Anabaena, Nostoc, Oscillatoria) that fix nitrogen. In paddy fields, cyanobacteria serve as an important biofertiliser.
