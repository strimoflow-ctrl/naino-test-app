🧪 1. Chemical Composition of Cells

Living organisms have a wide variety of organic and inorganic compounds. To find out the chemical composition, we perform a tissue analysis.

Analysis of Biomolecules

  • Take any living tissue (vegetable/liver). Grind it in trichloroacetic acid (Cl₃CCOOH) using mortar and pestle.
  • Filter the slurry through a cheesecloth or cotton. We get two fractions:
  • Acid-soluble pool (Filtrate): Contains micromolecules (molecular weight 18-800 Da). e.g., Amino acids, simple sugars, nucleotides.
  • Acid-insoluble fraction (Retentate): Contains macromolecules (molecular weight >10,000 Da). e.g., Proteins, Polysaccharides, Nucleic acids.
🚨 Exam Facts: Exception of Lipids

Lipids have a low molecular weight (rarely exceed 800 Da) but they come in the acid-insoluble fraction! Why? Because when we grind the tissue, cell membranes break and lipids form vesicles which are not water-soluble. So they get trapped with the macromolecules.

🧬 2. Types of Biomolecules

Amino Acids & Proteins

Amino acids are substituted methanes. There are 20 types of amino acids that make up proteins. They are linked by peptide bonds.

  • Primary structure: Linear sequence of amino acids.
  • Secondary structure: Right-handed α-helix and β-pleated sheet (due to hydrogen bonds).
  • Tertiary structure: Long protein chain folds upon itself like a hollow woolen ball (3D view). Necessary for many biological activities (like enzymes).
  • Quaternary structure: More than one polypeptide chain assemble together (e.g., Human Hemoglobin has 2 alpha and 2 beta subunits).
💡 Short Trick: Essential Amino Acids

PVT TIM HALL
Phenylalanine, Valine, Threonine, Tryptophan, Isoleucine, Methionine, Histidine, Arginine, Leucine, Lysine. (Must be obtained from diet).

Carbohydrates (Polysaccharides)

Long chains of sugars. Examples:

  • Cellulose: Homopolymer of glucose. Forms plant cell walls. Gives no color with Iodine because it lacks complex helices to hold I₂.
  • Starch: Homopolymer of glucose. Store house of energy in plants. Gives blue color with Iodine.
  • Glycogen: Animal starch. Found in liver and muscles. Right end is reducing, left end is non-reducing.
  • Inulin: Homopolymer of Fructose.

Nucleic Acids

Polynucleotides. A nucleotide consists of: Heterocyclic ring (nitrogenous base), Monosaccharide (pentose sugar), and Phosphoric acid/phosphate.

  • Purines: Adenine (A), Guanine (G). Have 2 rings.
  • Pyrimidines: Cytosine (C), Uracil (U), Thymine (T). Have 1 ring.
  • Bonds: Base to sugar = N-glycosidic linkage. Sugar to phosphate = Phosphoester bond. Between two nucleotides = 3'-5' phosphodiester bond.
🤯 Amazing Info: DNA Length

The DNA in a single human cell is about 2 meters long! To fit inside a microscopic nucleus, it is wound extremely tightly around proteins called histones.

⚡ 3. Enzymes

Almost all enzymes are proteins (exception: Ribozymes, which are RNA enzymes). Enzymes act as biological catalysts and lower the activation energy of a reaction.

Fig: Concept of Activation Energy showcasing how enzymes lower the energy barrier

Factors Affecting Enzyme Activity

  • Temperature and pH: Enzymes show highest activity at optimum temperature and pH. Activity declines both below and above the optimum value. Low temp preserves enzyme in a temporarily inactive state; high temp destroys it (denaturation).
  • Concentration of Substrate: With increase in substrate conc., velocity rises at first. It reaches a maximum velocity (Vmax) which is not exceeded by any further rise in substrate because enzyme molecules become fully saturated.
  • Inhibition: When binding of a chemical shuts off enzyme activity. Example of Competitive Inhibition: Inhibition of succinic dehydrogenase by malonate which closely resembles the substrate succinate.

Cofactors

Non-protein constituents bound to the enzyme to make it catalytically active. The protein portion is called apoenzyme.

  • Prosthetic groups: Organic compounds tightly bound to apoenzyme (e.g., Haem in peroxidase/catalase).
  • Co-enzymes: Organic compounds, transiently bound. Many vitamins serve as co-enzymes (e.g., NAD/NADP contain vitamin Niacin).
  • Metal Ions: Form coordination bonds. (e.g., Zinc is a cofactor for proteolytic enzyme carboxypeptidase).

Catalytic activity is lost when the co-factor is removed from the enzyme!
